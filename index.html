Why winners lose

In a common-value auction every bidder's estimate is the true value plus an error. The winner is usually the bidder with the largest error, so conditional on winning, the estimate is too high. With N bidders who all bid their estimate and errors uniform on ±e, the winner's expected overestimate is e·(N−1)/(N+1). With e = $20M and N = 6 that is about $14.3M, paid out of every win.

The curse grows with the number of bidders and with the noise in the estimates. Bidders who ignore it are not irrational about the value. They are naive about what winning tells them.

Shading (the expected-value fix)

Bid your estimate minus the expected overestimate given that you win. This is the Bayesian answer and earns the most in the simulation, but it needs a model of N and the noise.

Maximin (the worst-case fix)

Assume the true value is as low as your estimate allows, V = estimate − e, and choose the bid with the best worst-case payoff. Any bid at or below estimate − e never loses money, so maximin picks the highest of those. Losses disappear, but the rule wins rarely and leaves money on the table. The simulation shows it as safe and mediocre.

Minimax regret (the opportunity-cost fix)

Regret is the gap between your payoff and the best payoff you could have had in hindsight. Minimize the largest possible regret. The result depends on the scenarios you allow. If the highest rival bid can be anywhere near your estimate, regret is dominated by missed wins, and the rule recommends bidding your estimate: the curse stays. If rival bids are tied to the true value, the rule collapses to the maximin bid. Use this contrast to show that a rule can feel prudent and still ignore what winning reveals.

Structural remedies

Reserve prices, sharing information before bidding, ascending formats that let bidders learn from rivals' drop-outs, and contingent payments such as royalties all reduce the curse.

Debrief questions

  • Who won in round 1, and what did winning tell you about your estimate?
  • Why did the average winner lose more when we added computer bidders?
  • When the decision aids appeared, who followed them? Did profits improve or did participation collapse?
  • Where in your own firm do you see this pattern: acquisitions, hiring offers, vendor contracts, IPO allocations?

Suggested 45-minute run

  • 5 min: set up the lease scenario and share the link.
  • 10 min: classroom rounds 1 to 3 with decision aids off.
  • 5 min: reveal the leaderboard and the average winner profit.
  • 10 min: Live demo steps 1 and 2 to show the pattern is systematic.
  • 10 min: classroom rounds 4 to 6 with decision aids on, then the Strategy lab comparison.
  • 5 min: debrief.

Classroom notes

Students must open the shared link while signed in. Estimates are generated from a per-round seed, so a determined student can inspect the page and compute the true value. The game assumes good faith, as most classroom games do.
